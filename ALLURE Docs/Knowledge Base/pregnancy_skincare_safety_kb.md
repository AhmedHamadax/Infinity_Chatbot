## section 1

During pregnancy, skincare routines should generally be kept simple and focused on gentle cleansing, regular moisturizing, sun protection, and treatment of specific skin concerns only when necessary. Pregnancy can change how the skin behaves and may cause new acne, dryness, sensitivity, itching, or pigmentation even when these problems were not present before pregnancy.

## section 2

During pregnancy, a basic facial skincare routine can include a gentle cleanser, a moisturizer appropriate for the skin type, and a broad-spectrum sunscreen with an SPF of at least 30. Additional serums, exfoliants, acne treatments, or pigmentation treatments should only be added when they provide a clear benefit and contain ingredients appropriate for pregnancy.

## section 3

Pregnant people should review the ingredient lists of both prescription and over-the-counter skincare products rather than relying only on marketing claims such as “natural,” “clean,” or “pregnancy-friendly.” Some ingredients that require avoidance during pregnancy can be present in acne, anti-aging, pigmentation, or hair-loss products.

## section 4

A gentle cleanser can generally be used during pregnancy once or twice daily depending on skin type and tolerance. Lukewarm water and gentle cleansing are preferable because pregnancy may make the skin drier or more sensitive, and excessive washing or aggressive scrubbing can increase irritation.

## section 5

Moisturizing regularly is appropriate during pregnancy, particularly when hormonal changes cause dryness or increased skin sensitivity. A simple fragrance-free moisturizer can help support the skin barrier, and creams or ointments may be useful when pregnancy-related dryness is more pronounced.

## section 6

Daily sun protection is especially important during pregnancy because hormonal changes can increase the likelihood of melasma and other forms of hyperpigmentation. A broad-spectrum, water-resistant sunscreen with an SPF of at least 30 should be applied to exposed skin and combined with shade and sun-protective clothing when possible.

## section 7

Mineral sunscreens containing zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, or both are a useful sunscreen option during pregnancy, especially for skin that has become sensitive or easily irritated. Mineral sunscreen is often recommended as a simple pregnancy-friendly option and should still provide broad-spectrum protection with an SPF of at least 30.

## section 8

Melasma, sometimes called the “mask of pregnancy,” can appear as brown or gray-brown facial pigmentation during pregnancy. The most important skincare measure for pregnancy-related melasma is consistent sun protection because ultraviolet and visible-light exposure can make pigmentation darker or more persistent.

## section 9

Pregnancy-related hyperpigmentation should not automatically be treated with strong lightening products. Daily sunscreen, gentle skincare, and pregnancy-compatible ingredients should be prioritized, because some commonly used pigmentation treatments such as hydroquinone should be avoided during pregnancy.

## section 10

Hydroquinone should be avoided in skincare products during pregnancy. A pregnant person treating melasma, post-acne pigmentation, or other dark spots should choose alternative approaches rather than using hydroquinone-containing bleaching or lightening products.

## section 11

Vitamin C can be included in a pregnancy skincare routine when it is well tolerated. Topical vitamin C may be used as part of a routine addressing uneven skin tone or pregnancy-related pigmentation, but irritating formulations should be reduced or stopped if pregnancy has made the skin unusually sensitive.

## section 12

Glycolic acid can be used as a skincare ingredient during pregnancy and may help with acne, texture, or pigmentation. Because glycolic acid can still cause dryness or irritation, especially when pregnancy makes the skin more sensitive, it should be introduced gradually rather than combined immediately with several other exfoliating products.

## section 13

Acne can develop for the first time or become worse during pregnancy because of hormonal changes. Pregnancy-related acne should initially be managed with gentle cleansing, oil-free or noncomedogenic products, avoidance of picking or squeezing lesions, and acne ingredients that are considered appropriate for use during pregnancy.

## section 14

Azelaic acid is considered an appropriate topical treatment option for acne during pregnancy. Azelaic acid can also be useful when acne is accompanied by post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, making it a practical pregnancy-compatible ingredient when both breakouts and acne-related dark spots are concerns.

## section 15

Topical benzoyl peroxide can be used for acne during pregnancy, but a conservative approach is appropriate. Benzoyl peroxide should generally be used in limited amounts, and a pregnant person who requires frequent, high-strength, or extensive application should discuss the treatment with an obstetrician or dermatologist.

## section 16

Topical salicylic acid can be used for acne during pregnancy, but concentration, treatment area, and duration of use should be considered. High-strength salicylic acid products, particularly concentrations above approximately 2%, extensive application, or prolonged use should not be started during pregnancy without discussing the treatment with a healthcare professional.

## section 17

Pregnancy is not an appropriate time to aggressively combine multiple exfoliating or acne treatments simply to obtain faster results. Combining salicylic acid, glycolic acid, benzoyl peroxide, and other potentially irritating ingredients may cause excessive dryness, burning, or inflammation even when the individual ingredients themselves may be acceptable during pregnancy.

## section 18

Retinoids should be avoided in skincare and acne treatments during pregnancy. This precaution applies to vitamin A–derived retinoid ingredients used for acne, pigmentation, texture, or anti-aging purposes because safer alternative skincare options are available during pregnancy.

## section 19

Retinol-containing cosmetic and anti-aging products should be discontinued during pregnancy. The fact that retinol is available without a prescription does not make it an appropriate pregnancy skincare ingredient, and pregnant users should check anti-aging creams, serums, eye products, and acne products for retinol or related retinoid ingredients.

## section 20

Topical tretinoin should be avoided during pregnancy. Although absorption from topical tretinoin is much lower than with oral isotretinoin, expert guidance generally recommends discontinuing topical tretinoin during pregnancy and using alternative treatments for acne or pigmentation.

## section 21

Adapalene should be avoided during pregnancy even though it is available over the counter in some countries. Adapalene is a topical retinoid, and pregnancy recommendations generally advise stopping adapalene and choosing a non-retinoid acne treatment such as azelaic acid when appropriate.

## section 22

Tazarotene should not be used during pregnancy. Tazarotene is a topical retinoid and is among the acne and anti-aging medications that should be stopped when pregnancy occurs rather than continued as part of a skincare routine.

## section 23

Oral isotretinoin must not be used during pregnancy because fetal exposure can cause severe birth defects and other serious pregnancy complications. A person who becomes pregnant while taking isotretinoin or suspects pregnancy during isotretinoin treatment should stop the medication and contact the prescribing clinician and obstetric healthcare professional promptly.

## section 24

Spironolactone should not be used to treat acne during pregnancy. Although spironolactone is commonly prescribed for hormonal acne outside pregnancy, its hormonal effects make it inappropriate during pregnancy, so another acne treatment should be selected with a healthcare professional.

## section 25

Hormonal anti-acne medications that work by blocking androgen activity should not be used as acne treatments during pregnancy unless specifically managed by an appropriate healthcare professional. Pregnancy-related acne should instead be treated with pregnancy-compatible topical options whenever possible.

## section 26

Tetracycline-family oral antibiotics used for acne, including tetracycline, doxycycline, and minocycline, are not routine acne treatments for pregnancy. These medicines require pregnancy-specific restrictions because exposure later in pregnancy can affect developing teeth and bones, so pregnant people should not start or continue these antibiotics for acne without medical guidance.

## section 27

Prescription acne treatment during pregnancy should be reviewed with an obstetrician or dermatologist rather than continued automatically from a pre-pregnancy routine. Some topical prescription treatments, such as topical clindamycin, may be considered during pregnancy, while other acne medications such as retinoids, spironolactone, and isotretinoin should be avoided.

## section 28

A pregnant person who discovers that a previously used skincare product contains retinol, adapalene, tretinoin, or another topical retinoid should discontinue the product and discuss the exposure with the obstetric healthcare professional. Accidental topical exposure should be evaluated based on the actual product and exposure rather than making assumptions about pregnancy risk.

## section 29

Pregnant people should avoid starting prescription-strength pigmentation, anti-aging, acne, eczema, psoriasis, or hair-loss medications without reviewing them with the clinician managing the pregnancy. Pregnancy safety varies substantially between medications, and a treatment that was appropriate before pregnancy may need to be changed, reduced, or discontinued.

## section 30

Finasteride should be avoided during pregnancy. Finasteride may be found in treatments for hair loss rather than facial skincare, so pregnant people reviewing personal-care medications should check hair and scalp treatments in addition to facial skincare products.

## section 31

Pregnancy can make previously well-tolerated skin more sensitive, dry, or reactive. When burning, stinging, redness, or excessive dryness develops, the skincare routine should be simplified and strong exfoliants, unnecessary active ingredients, fragranced products, and aggressive cleansing should be reduced until the skin barrier recovers.

## section 32

Fragrance-free skincare products can be useful during pregnancy when the skin becomes sensitive, itchy, or reactive. Products labeled “unscented” are not necessarily equivalent to “fragrance-free,” because an unscented product may still contain ingredients added to mask other odors.

## section 33

Pregnant people should be cautious about assuming that essential oils or botanical skincare ingredients are automatically safer because they are natural. Some essential oils and plant extracts can irritate the skin, and safety information for concentrated essential oils during pregnancy may be limited, so unnecessary concentrated essential-oil products are best discussed with a healthcare professional.

## section 34

A conservative pregnancy skincare routine can minimize unnecessary exposure to ingredients such as phthalates, parabens, triclosan, and strongly fragranced products when practical. These ingredients are different from clearly contraindicated medications such as retinoids or isotretinoin; avoiding unnecessary exposure is a precautionary approach rather than evidence that every product containing these ingredients will cause pregnancy complications.

## section 35

Stretch marks commonly develop during pregnancy as the skin expands. Moisturizers can help keep stretched skin soft and may reduce dryness or itching, but topical creams, oils, or cosmetic products have not been proven to reliably prevent or remove pregnancy stretch marks.

## section 36

Pregnancy-related abdominal skin that feels dry, tight, or mildly itchy can generally be managed with gentle cleansing and regular use of a fragrance-free moisturizer. Persistent or severe itching during pregnancy should not automatically be assumed to be ordinary dry skin because some pregnancy-specific medical conditions can also cause significant itching.

## section 37

Severe itching during pregnancy without an obvious rash, particularly itching involving the palms of the hands or soles of the feet and worsening at night, requires prompt assessment by an obstetric healthcare professional. This pattern can occur with intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, a pregnancy-related liver condition that requires medical evaluation rather than treatment with skincare products alone.

## section 38

A new widespread, intensely itchy rash during pregnancy should be medically evaluated when symptoms are severe, persistent, rapidly spreading, or associated with significant discomfort. Pregnancy can be associated with specific skin disorders that may require treatment beyond moisturizers or over-the-counter skincare.

## section 39

Blisters that develop on the abdomen or spread over a large area during pregnancy should be assessed by a healthcare professional. Blistering during pregnancy can occasionally indicate a pregnancy-specific skin disorder such as pemphigoid gestationis and should not be treated only as ordinary sensitive skin or an allergic cosmetic reaction.

## section 40

A pre-existing skin condition such as eczema, psoriasis, rosacea, or severe acne can improve or worsen during pregnancy. Pregnant people with chronic skin conditions should review their existing medications with both the dermatologist and obstetric healthcare professional rather than stopping all treatment automatically, because some therapies can be continued safely while others require replacement.

## section 41

Pregnancy skincare should prioritize symptom control and skin health rather than aggressive cosmetic treatment. Elective treatment of fine lines, mild pigmentation, texture, or other cosmetic concerns can often be simplified or postponed when the preferred treatment would require an ingredient that is not recommended during pregnancy.

## section 42

When evaluating a skincare recommendation for a pregnant person, the most important safety distinction is whether the ingredient is clearly contraindicated, acceptable with normal topical use, appropriate only in limited amounts, or insufficiently studied. Pregnancy recommendations should not describe every cosmetic ingredient as either completely safe or completely dangerous when available evidence supports a more cautious or conditional recommendation.

## section 43

A practical pregnancy skincare routine for uncomplicated skin can consist of a gentle cleanser, moisturizer, and broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher in the morning, followed by gentle cleansing and moisturizer in the evening. If treatment is required, ingredients such as azelaic acid or glycolic acid can be considered according to the skin concern and tolerance, while retinoids and hydroquinone should be avoided.

## section 44

A pregnancy skincare routine for acne can prioritize gentle cleansing, noncomedogenic moisturizer, sunscreen, and pregnancy-compatible acne treatments such as azelaic acid. Benzoyl peroxide or topical salicylic acid may also be considered with appropriate limits, while adapalene, tretinoin, tazarotene, oral isotretinoin, spironolactone, and unsuitable systemic acne medications should not be used without pregnancy-specific medical guidance.

## section 45

A pregnancy skincare routine for melasma or dark spots should prioritize daily broad-spectrum sunscreen and gentle pigmentation management rather than aggressive bleaching treatments. Vitamin C or glycolic acid may be considered when tolerated, while hydroquinone and topical retinoids should be avoided during pregnancy.

## section 46

A pregnant person should contact an obstetrician, dermatologist, or other qualified healthcare professional when a skincare concern requires prescription treatment, when the safety of an ingredient is uncertain, when acne becomes severe or scarring, when a chronic skin condition worsens, or when a skincare reaction causes substantial redness, swelling, blistering, pain, or persistent inflammation.
# Skin Types

Skin type describes the skin's general baseline behavior rather than a specific skincare problem. It should be used to adjust product texture, cleansing intensity, moisturization, and routine tolerance.

Skin type should not replace assessment of the user's actual skincare concern. A person with oily skin can still have dehydration or irritation, and a person with dry skin can still develop acne.

Product selection should therefore consider both the main skincare concern and the user's skin type.

---

## Oily Skin

Oily skin produces noticeable excess sebum and may appear shiny, especially across the forehead, nose, and chin. Users may describe persistent greasiness, makeup slipping quickly, enlarged-looking pores, or the need to blot the face during the day.

The goal is not to completely remove skin oil. Excessively stripping the skin can increase discomfort and interfere with the skin barrier. Oily skin generally benefits from effective but non-aggressive cleansing, lightweight hydration, and products that do not leave an unnecessarily heavy or greasy finish.

When oily skin is accompanied by acne, clogged pores, or blackheads, the acne concern should determine the primary treatment rather than selecting products based only on oiliness.

## Oily Skin Without Acne

Oily skin does not automatically mean acne-prone skin.

A user may have visible shine or excess sebum without blackheads, inflammatory pimples, or recurrent breakouts. In this situation, skincare should focus on comfortable cleansing, lightweight hydration, and suitable sunscreen rather than introducing acne treatment unnecessarily.

## Oily Skin That Feels Dehydrated

Oily skin can still become dehydrated.

The user may describe a shiny or oily face while also experiencing tightness, reduced plumpness, or discomfort. Oil production and water content are different characteristics, so the presence of excess sebum does not mean that hydration should be avoided.

The appropriate approach is lightweight hydration without relying exclusively on heavy moisturizing textures.

A lightweight moisturizer can be added when necessary.

## Dry Skin

Dry skin generally produces less natural surface oil and may feel persistently tight, rough, flaky, or uncomfortable. Dryness can become more noticeable after washing or in dry weather.

The main goal is to support moisture retention and the skin barrier. Cleansers should avoid unnecessarily stripping the skin, and regular moisturization is usually more important than frequent exfoliation or oil-control products.

A dry skin type should be distinguished from temporary dehydration. Dry skin is a general baseline tendency, while dehydration can temporarily occur in any skin type.

## Combination Skin

Combination skin has different behavior in different facial areas.

The most common pattern is greater oiliness or shine through the T-zone — forehead, nose, and chin — while the cheeks are normal or drier.

The routine should avoid treating the entire face as extremely oily or extremely dry. Lightweight, balanced products are usually easier to use across the face, while targeted products can be applied to specific areas when necessary.

Do not automatically classify combination skin as oily skin based only on an oily nose or forehead.

## Combination Skin With Oily T-Zone and Dry Cheeks

A common combination-skin pattern involves a shiny forehead and nose together with relatively dry or comfortable cheeks.

Using aggressive oil-control treatment over the entire face can unnecessarily dry the cheek area.

The routine should therefore remain balanced, with oil-control products focused on areas where they are actually needed and moisturization adjusted according to regional dryness.

Lightweight hydration: Hyalu Collagen Serum.

Moisturizer can be applied more generously to dry areas when required.

Oil-control products should not automatically be applied aggressively over already dry areas.

## Normal Skin

Normal skin is generally balanced, without persistent excessive oiliness, marked dryness, frequent tightness, or significant reactivity.

The main skincare goal is usually maintenance rather than correcting the skin type itself.

A normal skin type does not mean that the user has no skincare concerns. Normal skin can still develop acne, pigmentation, fine lines, dark circles, or other problems that require targeted treatment.

Infinity product considerations

General cleansing: Facial Cleanser.

General moisturization when needed: Moisturizing Cream or another suitable moisturizer.

General sun protection: Sunscreen Cream SPF50+ all Skin Types or another sunscreen selected according to user preference.

Treatment products should be chosen according to the actual concern rather than the normal skin type.

## Sensitive Skin

Sensitive skin is characterized by increased tendency toward irritation or discomfort from skincare products or environmental exposure.

Users may describe frequent burning, stinging, redness, itching, or a history of reacting easily to skincare products.

Sensitivity does not indicate whether the skin is oily or dry. A user may have oily-sensitive, dry-sensitive, combination-sensitive, or otherwise reactive skin.

For this reason, sensitivity should mainly influence product tolerance and routine intensity rather than completely replacing the user's other skincare characteristics.

## Dry Skin Versus Dehydrated Skin

Dry skin and dehydrated skin are related but not identical.

Dry skin describes a baseline tendency toward insufficient surface lipids or natural oil and may cause persistent roughness, flaking, or tightness.

Dehydrated skin describes insufficient water content and can occur temporarily in oily, dry, combination, or normal skin.

This distinction matters because a user who says their normally oily skin currently feels tight should not automatically be reclassified as having a dry skin type.

Infinity product considerations

For dehydration and loss of plumpness: Hyalu Collagen Serum.

For persistent dry skin requiring a cream: Moisturizing Cream or Hyalu Collagen Cream.

For dryness with irritation or barrier damage: Cica Repair Cream.

## Oily Skin Versus Acne-Prone Skin

Oily skin describes sebum production, while acne describes a separate concern involving clogged pores, comedones, inflammatory lesions, or other acne patterns.

The two frequently occur together but are not interchangeable.

A user with oily skin does not automatically require acne treatment, and acne can occur in people who do not describe their skin as oily.

Infinity product considerations

Oiliness alone: focus on suitable cleansing, lightweight hydration, and sunscreen finish.

Actual acne: use the Acne knowledge sections to select treatment according to lesion pattern.

Do not infer acne from oily_skin alone.

---

## Sensitive Skin Versus Irritated Skin

Sensitive skin generally describes a tendency to react easily over time.

Irritated skin describes a current state and can occur even in someone who does not normally consider their skin sensitive.

The distinction is useful because current irritation may require temporary barrier-focused care, whereas a history of sensitivity mainly affects treatment tolerability.

Infinity product considerations

Current irritation or barrier disruption: Cica Repair Cream, Panthenol Cream 5%, or another appropriate soothing product.

Sensitive skin without active irritation: treat the user's primary concern while accounting for tolerability separately.

---

## Skin Type Should Not Determine the Entire Recommendation

Skin type is contextual information, not the primary diagnosis of the user's skincare problem.

The main treatment should normally be selected according to the user's concern, symptoms, and treatment goal.

Skin type should then help refine factors such as cleanser strength, moisturizer texture, oil-control needs, and tolerance.

Infinity product match

Do not recommend a product simply because it is marketed for a particular skin type if it does not address the user's actual concern.

A user with oily skin asking about fine lines should still receive fine-line treatment.

A user with dry skin asking about acne should still have the acne pattern evaluated.

A user with sensitive skin asking about pigmentation should still have the pigmentation treatment scientifically identified, followed by appropriate safety and tolerability assessment.
